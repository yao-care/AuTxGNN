# Diazepam: From Epilepsy and Anxiety to Insomnia

## One-Sentence Summary

Diazepam is a benzodiazepine originally used for epilepsy, status epilepticus, anxiety, alcohol withdrawal and tetanus.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be useful for **insomnia**, but there are **no registered clinical trials** and the **20 publications** compiled are mostly indirect (reviews, herbal medicine and animal studies, and safety or dependence papers).

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Diazepam is a benzodiazepine, a class that enhances GABA-A receptor inhibitory signalling. This produces sedative-hypnotic, anxiolytic and anticonvulsant effects.

Its established uses (anxiety, seizures, alcohol withdrawal) all rely on calming central nervous system activity, and anxiety and insomnia often occur together. Benzodiazepines are already used for short-term insomnia, so the prediction is mechanistically plausible.

However, the supporting literature is largely indirect. It consists mainly of reviews, herbal and animal studies, and papers on dependence, tapering and sleep architecture. A 1981 double-blind study (PMID 6113175) compared diazepam 5 mg with lormetazepam in 100 outpatients with insomnia, but it found lormetazepam to be better. The data also flag a long-term safety signal (see below).

Other lower-ranked predictions (all Hold) were also reviewed:
- **Sleep disorder (initiating and maintaining sleep)**: Evidence level L4. The literature focuses on benzodiazepine deprescribing and harms rather than diazepam efficacy.
- **Barbiturate abuse and hallucinogen abuse**: Evidence level L4. Diazepam could plausibly manage withdrawal or agitation through GABA-A cross-tolerance, but the literature is toxicology, abuse liability and alcohol withdrawal management, with no direct trials.
- **Antidepressant type abuse**: Evidence level L4. The disease label looks mismatched, and the literature concerns benzodiazepine dependence itself.
- **Specific developmental disorder and ADHD (two entries)**: Evidence level L4 for developmental disorder and ADHD, L5 for ADHD inattentive type. Mechanistic rationale is weak, and a report of paradoxical reactions in children with autism (PMID 2826308) raises a paediatric safety concern.
- **Cauda equina syndrome and obsolete neurogenic bladder**: Evidence level L5. These are model predictions only, with no trials or literature.

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindications (from the knowledge graph, not label text)**: liver failure and liver disease, gallbladder disease, open-angle and angle-closure glaucoma, sleep apnoea syndromes, respiratory failure and lung disease, congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, myasthenia gravis and other neuromuscular junction disease, substance-related disorders and dependence, and anxiety-related disorders (anxiety disorder, neurotic disorder, dysthymic disorder).
- **Drug Interactions**: 100 interactions were found. Notable examples:
  - **Major**: morphine (additive central nervous system and respiratory depression).
  - **Moderate**: bupropion, aprepitant, omeprazole, esomeprazole, cimetidine, clarithromycin, cisapride, clotrimazole, miconazole, dronabinol and metoclopramide.
  - **Minor**: dexamethasone, betamethasone and several antacids.
- **Long-term use concerns from the literature**: dependence, withdrawal, altered sleep architecture in older adults, and an observed association with breast cancer risk (PMID 40583063). Abuse liability of diazepam is also documented.

Please also refer to the TGA-approved Product Information (PI) for full warnings and contraindications.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The TxGNN score is very high, but there are no registered trials and no diazepam-specific efficacy data for insomnia. The existing evidence is indirect and highlights harms (dependence, cognitive effects, sleep disruption), and other benzodiazepines and hypnotics are already established for short-term insomnia.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanism of action data (MOA) and similarity analysis to the original indications
- The TGA Product Information, to confirm approved indications, warnings and contraindications (the licence text is currently blank)
- Direct comparative or controlled trial evidence of diazepam for insomnia, including against current first-line options (cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, short-acting hypnotics)
- A risk assessment of dependence, tolerance, falls and long-term safety, particularly in older adults
- Manual curation of the literature, since relevance screening is still pending for most publications
