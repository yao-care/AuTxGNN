# Apomorphine: From Parkinson's Disease to Schizophrenia

## One-Sentence Summary

Apomorphine is a dopamine agonist originally used to treat Parkinson's disease.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **schizophrenia**, but only **indirect, mostly historical literature (19 publications)** and **no registered clinical trials** currently relate to this direction.
The predicted effect runs against the usual treatment logic, so this remains a research question rather than a ready candidate.

*Note: The top-ranked TxGNN prediction (polymicrogyria, perisylvian, with cerebellar hypoplasia and arthrogryposis) has no trials or literature and no identifiable mechanistic link. This report therefore focuses on the highest-ranked prediction with literature, schizophrenia (rank 5).*

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Apomorphine is known as a direct-acting D1/D2 dopamine receptor agonist, and its efficacy in Parkinson's disease is established. The dopamine system is also central to the dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia, so the target system is plausibly relevant. This is likely what drives the model's prediction.

The direction of effect is the main concern. The dopamine hypothesis implicates enhanced dopamine function in positive symptoms, and established antipsychotics block D2 receptors. A dopamine agonist would therefore be expected to worsen psychosis. A 2001 review (PMID 11394190) describes this as a "dilemma", finding little evidence that apomorphine at doses stimulating postsynaptic receptors induces psychosis. Some older work explored low-dose effects mediated through dopamine autoreceptors, which might reduce dopamine activity.

The older studies mainly used apomorphine as a probe of dopamine receptor sensitivity, not as a therapy. Where treatment was tested, results were negative or inconclusive (see below). The prediction is therefore best viewed as a hypothesis, not a supported repurposing opportunity.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
- The schizophrenia prediction has no registered trials. The few historical treatment studies (1983–1984) found no significant benefit, and a dopamine agonist is expected to risk worsening psychosis. The knowledge graph also lists psychotic disorders as contraindications.
- The other nine predictions have no trials or literature, and most have no identifiable mechanistic link.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanism of action data (e.g. from DrugBank)
- TGA Product Information with warnings and contraindications, and the approved indication text for the ARTG entry
- A resolution of the safety question of psychosis worsening, for example through a review of the older low-dose autoreceptor hypothesis
- A justification for any new route or formulation, since only a subcutaneous injectable is marketed in Australia
- Only if a credible rationale emerges: a carefully designed early-phase study with close psychiatric monitoring
