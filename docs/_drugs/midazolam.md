# Midazolam: From Anxiety to Insomnia

## One-Sentence Summary

Midazolam is a short-acting benzodiazepine (GABA-A modulator), originally used for anxiety and sedation.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **insomnia**, supported by **2 registered clinical trials** (neither tests midazolam for chronic insomnia) and **10 publications**, including several older randomised hypnotic studies.
This is closer to re-confirming a known hypnotic use than to a novel repurposing.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available in the data compiled for this report. Midazolam is a benzodiazepine that enhances GABA-A receptor inhibition. This gives it potent sedative, hypnotic and anxiolytic effects, so a sleep indication is mechanistically consistent with its original use for anxiety.

Midazolam was studied orally as a hypnotic in the 1980s and 1990s. Studies included a dose-finding trial and a 14-day comparison with flurazepam in chronic insomniacs. It is also reported to be used as a hypnotic or sedative in some countries.

Its short half-life gives rapid onset but may limit sleep maintenance. Tolerance, dependence, next-day impairment and CYP3A4-mediated interactions remain concerns.

## Safety Considerations

Product Information warnings and contraindications were not available in the data compiled for this report. Please refer to the TGA-approved Product Information (PI) for these.

- **Disease-level cautions (from knowledge base associations, not label text):** chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cirrhosis of the liver, angle-closure glaucoma and chronic kidney disease or end-stage renal failure.
- **Drug Interactions:** 100 interactions were recorded. The main moderate-level examples are:
  - Opioid and sedative combinations: morphine, dronabinol and nabilone.
  - Possible metabolic interactions: clarithromycin, miconazole, clotrimazole, aprepitant, cimetidine, ranitidine, omeprazole and bupropion.
  - Other moderate interactions: metoclopramide, eluxadoline, teduglutide and glycerol phenylbutyrate.
  - Minor interactions include dexamethasone, betamethasone, magnesium oxide, aluminium hydroxide and calcium carbonate.
- **General concerns noted in the evidence review:** tolerance, dependence, next-day impairment and CYP3A4 interactions.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The mechanism is plausible, and older randomised studies suggest oral midazolam works as a hypnotic. However, the evidence is dated and the registered trials do not address chronic insomnia. The only ARTG listing is an injectable, and safety labelling data are missing. Current evidence supports this as a research question, not a clinical recommendation.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Product Information warnings and contraindications for Australia.
- Mechanism of action data.
- Confirmation of route and formulation (oral vs injectable) and ARTG indication wording.
- Contemporary comparative trials against current insomnia treatments, with attention to dependence and next-day impairment.
- A plan for managing CYP3A4 and CNS-depressant interactions.
