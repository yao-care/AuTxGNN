# Citric Acid: From Dyspepsia and Headache Disorder to Stomach Disease

## One-Sentence Summary

Citric acid was originally associated with dyspepsia and headache disorder.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **stomach disease**,
but **no clinical trials** are registered and the **20 publications** retrieved offer only indirect support, so this remains a research question rather than an established use.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Currently, detailed mechanism of action data is not available. Based on known information, citric acid is already used in dyspepsia-related products, and its acidifying effect on gastric pH is plausibly relevant to gastric physiology. Mechanistically it may be applicable to stomach disease, but this is a hypothesis only.

The literature supports this link only indirectly. One diagnostic study used a citric acid test meal to improve the accuracy of the 13C-urea breath test for *Helicobacter pylori*. That is a diagnostic adjunct, not a treatment. Most other retrieved items are off-topic (cuproptosis in gastric cancer, cobalamin deficiency in animals, metabolomics, obesity) or general reviews.

The very high TxGNN score is a computational prediction. It is not backed by therapeutic evidence. It also does not distinguish between a possible benefit and the opposite: citric acid could worsen some acid-related conditions (see Safety Considerations).

## Clinical Trial Evidence

## Safety Considerations

- **Contraindications flagged in the knowledge graph (not label text):** hypertension, kidney disease and impaired renal function, anuria, liver disease, gallbladder disease, congestive heart failure, adrenal insufficiency (including Addison disease and congenital adrenal hyperplasia), peptic ulcer disease, gout, hypercalcaemia, nephrocalcinosis, phosphorus metabolism disease, thrombocytopenia and thrombocytopenic purpura, haemophilia, anaemia, chronic diarrhoeal disease, and substance abuse/dependence. These come from a computational knowledge graph and need confirmation against the product labelling. The peptic ulcer disease flag is directly relevant to the predicted gastric indication.
- **Drug interactions (15 identified):**
  - **Major:** aluminium hydroxide. Citrate can increase aluminium absorption.
  - **Moderate:** fluoroquinolones, including ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin, norfloxacin, ofloxacin, enoxacin, gatifloxacin, gemifloxacin, lomefloxacin, grepafloxacin, sparfloxacin and trovafloxacin, as well as cinoxacin and nalidixic acid.

Product Information warnings and contraindications were not available for this report. Please refer to the TGA-approved Product Information (PI) for label safety information.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The prediction rests on a very high model score but has no registered clinical trials and only indirect literature, mostly diagnostic or off-topic. Knowledge-graph flags for peptic ulcer disease and renal, hepatic and cardiovascular conditions add caution. Nothing in the data supports moving beyond a research question.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanism of action data for citric acid
- Product Information warnings and contraindications from the TGA/PBS sources
- Product details for the ARTG entry (name, dosage form, approved indication)
- Controlled clinical evidence, or at least a structured literature review, for gastric disease outcomes
- A safety review addressing the interaction with aluminium-containing antacids and the contraindication flags above

**Other predicted indications:** The remaining predictions (pharyngitis, acute laryngopharyngitis, postgastrectomy syndrome, nasal cavity disease, papillary conjunctivitis, blepharoconjunctivitis, rhinitis, thrombotic disease, ulcerative blepharitis) have equally weak support. Most have no literature at all, and the rhinitis and thrombotic disease evidence is indirect (an animal sinonasal biofilm model and a small ex vivo platelet study). None changes the Hold recommendation.
