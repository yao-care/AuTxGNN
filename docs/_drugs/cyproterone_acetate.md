## Cyproterone Acetate: From Androgen-Dependent Conditions to Migraine Disorder
{: .text-alpha }

## One-Sentence Summary

Cyproterone acetate is a synthetic antiandrogen and progestogen, used for androgen-dependent conditions such as prostate cancer, acne and hirsutism, and in some combined oral contraceptives.
The TxGNN model predicts it may be effective for **migraine disorder**, but **no clinical trials** and only **3 indirect publications** (all reviews or cohort studies) support this prediction, and none shows that the drug treats migraine.

## Why is This Prediction Reasonable?

Detailed mechanism of action data is not available. Based on known pharmacology, cyproterone acetate is an antiandrogen with progestogenic activity. It acts on the androgen receptor and modulates sex-steroid signalling.

Migraine is hormone-sensitive, with menstrual and perimenopausal triggers. This is the only plausible link to the drug. The retrieved literature shows that progestins have differential effects on the brain, including interactions with GABA-A receptors, dopaminergic responses and opioid receptor binding. It also shows that hormone supplementation affects migraine variably in postmenopausal women. These findings are indirect, and none demonstrates that cyproterone acetate prevents or treats migraine.

The high score comes from the TxGNN model (deep learning) and may reflect similarities the model learned between related diseases, not biological evidence or clinical efficacy. One paper on long-term side effects of cyproterone acetate therapy may even point to headache as an adverse effect.

Related predictions deserve caution. The model also ranks migraine with brainstem aura and migraine susceptibility highly. The literature for these concerns hormonal contraception and vascular or thrombotic risk. Migraine with aura is a recognised risk modifier for combined hormonal contraceptives because of ischaemic stroke risk. This is a safety signal, not a therapeutic rationale.

## Safety Considerations

Product Information warnings and contraindications were not available in the data compiled for this report. Please refer to the TGA-approved Product Information (PI) for full safety information.

Safety signals from the retrieved literature:
- **Thrombosis:** Cyproterone acetate-containing combined oral contraceptives are associated with prothrombotic changes and a higher risk of venous thromboembolism, particularly with factor V Leiden.
- **Aura and stroke risk:** Combined hormonal contraceptives carry ischaemic stroke and vascular risk, which is relevant in migraine with aura.
- **Long-term effects:** Published discussion of mutagenic potential and other long-term adverse effects.
- **Drug interactions:** The only interaction record is pharmacological, showing antagonism at the androgen receptor (AR). No clinically graded drug-drug interactions were identified.

## Conclusion and Next Steps

**Decision: Hold**

**Rationale:**
The migraine prediction rests on model output and indirect hormonal literature. There are no clinical trials and no evidence of benefit, and the thrombotic and vascular safety signals argue against use in migraine, especially with aura.

**To proceed, the following is needed:**
- Mechanism of action data confirming a plausible link to migraine pathophysiology
- Preclinical or early-phase clinical evidence of benefit in migraine
- Review of the TGA Product Information for warnings and contraindications
- A benefit-risk assessment addressing thromboembolic and stroke risk, particularly in patients with aura

**Other predictions in the data (for context):**
- Amenorrhoea (L3) reflects an established, regulated use of cyproterone acetate with ethinylestradiol in hyperandrogenic menstrual disorders, so it is not a novel repurposing candidate. One Phase IV trial is registered ([NCT01103518](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01103518), 100 participants, status unknown).
- Thrombophilia, factor V excess, antithrombin deficiency and heparin cofactor 2 deficiency are better read as risk flags than therapeutic opportunities.
- Prinzmetal angina has no supporting evidence.
- Breast fibrocystic disease has only old, small, non-randomised studies.
